# A day in the life of a software engineer at a mid-size company

People considering a switch into software usually imagine the hobby version:
headphones on, flow state, building something cool. The job version overlaps
with that, but it is not that. Here is a realistic Tuesday for a mid-level
engineer at a roughly 300-person company — not a flashy startup, not
faang, just a normal software business.

**8:45** — Coffee, triage. Skim Slack and email. Two messages from product
about an edge case in yesterday's release. A reminder that the sprint
demo is Thursday. A failing test on the main branch that someone needs to
look at.

**9:15** — Standup. Fifteen minutes, ideally. Each person says what they did
yesterday, what they're doing today, and what's blocking them. The honest
content of standup is mostly "still working on the thing I said yesterday."

**9:30** — Real work begins, or tries to. You pick up a ticket: add filtering
to a customer-facing report. Sounds small. You spend the first 40 minutes just
*understanding the existing code* — someone wrote it two years ago, the person
is gone, and the comments lie. This is the part nobody tells hobbyists about:
most professional software work is reading and modifying code you didn't
write, under constraints you didn't choose.

**11:00** — Code review. A teammate's pull request needs eyes. You leave six
comments, three substantive, three nitpicks you flag as optional. Then you
context-switch back to your own ticket and lose ten minutes remembering where
you were.

**12:30** — Lunch, maybe at your desk reading the news, maybe with coworkers.

**1:30** — A meeting that could have been an email. Then a *useful* meeting:
the team disagrees about how to model a new feature in the database, and the
discussion genuinely changes the design for the better. Both kinds of meetings
are the job.

**3:00** — Back in the code. You hit the satisfying part — the filtering works
locally, the tests pass, you open a PR. For twenty minutes it feels exactly
like the hobby. Then CI fails on a flaky integration test that has nothing to
do with your change, and you spend half an hour proving it's not your fault.

**4:30** — A production alert. Latency spiked on an endpoint. It turns out to
be a slow database query someone shipped this morning. Not your code, but
you're the one online, so you help dig. This is "on-call" energy even when it's
not formally your week.

**5:30** — Wind down. Update the ticket, leave notes for tomorrow, log off —
or don't, if there's a deadline.

The texture that surprises career changers: the coding itself is maybe
40-50% of the day. The rest is communication, reading legacy code, reviews,
meetings, and the small frictions of working inside a system other people
built. The people who thrive tend to be the ones who *also* enjoy that
connective tissue — the puzzle of a messy real-world system — and not only the
clean dopamine of a greenfield side project. If the only part you love is the
greenfield side project at midnight with no one watching, that is worth
knowing before you bet on the job.
