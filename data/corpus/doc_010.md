# A 12-month skill-building plan template for a working career changer

This is a template, not a prescription. It assumes the hardest case: you have a
demanding full-time job and a young child, so your study time is scarce and
fragmented — maybe 6 to 10 hours a week, mostly at night. The goal is to reach
*credible junior hireability* in roughly a year without quitting your job
first, and to do it in a way that also tells you whether you even want this.

## Phase 1 (months 1-3): Foundations and a forcing function

- Pick **one language and stick to it.** For web, Python or JavaScript. Resist
  the urge to sample five.
- Get genuinely comfortable with the fundamentals: data structures, control
  flow, functions, and one framework (e.g., a Python web framework on the
  backend).
- **Escape tutorial purgatory early.** The single biggest failure mode of
  self-study is endlessly consuming tutorials without building. By the end of
  month 3, you should have built one small thing *from scratch*, badly. Badly
  is fine. From scratch is the point.
- Set up the professional tooling now: version control, a public code-hosting
  profile, a basic command-line workflow. These are part of the job, not
  separate from it.

## Phase 2 (months 4-7): Build the portfolio project

- Choose **one real, non-trivial project** and commit to shipping it deployed
  and public. One polished project beats five tutorial clones.
- Strongly consider a project that leverages your existing domain. A career
  changer from a technical field who builds something in their old domain
  signals "I bring knowledge you don't have," not "I'm a generic beginner."
- Learn by *needing* things: pick up the front-end framework, the database, the
  deployment platform because your project requires them, not in the abstract.
- Ship it for real — deployed, with a clear README explaining what it does and
  why. A live URL is worth more than a stack of certificates.

## Phase 3 (months 8-10): Reality-test the work and the field

- **Pressure-test "hobby vs. job."** Contribute to an open-source issue and go
  through a real review cycle. Take one small freelance task. Shadow a working
  engineer for a day. The point is to experience software as *work* — deadlines,
  review, someone else's messy code — before betting on it. If you still like
  it, that's enormously valuable signal. If you don't, you just saved yourself a
  far costlier discovery.
- Begin networking in earnest: a local meetup, reconnecting with contacts in
  the field, an informational coffee. Most first jobs come through people, not
  cold applications.

## Phase 4 (months 11-12): Job-search readiness

- Polish the portfolio and write up the project as a story you can tell.
- Practice explaining your *thinking*, not just your code — communication is
  weighted heavily for career changers.
- Do a focused round of interview prep (fundamentals, a moderate amount of
  problem-solving practice).
- Apply selectively and warmly (through your network) rather than blasting cold
  applications. Quality of targeting beats quantity.

## Built-in checkpoints

At months 3, 7, and 10, stop and ask honestly: *Am I progressing? Do I still
want this? Is the pace sustainable alongside my job and family, or am I burning
out?* The plan has off-ramps on purpose. If the answer at month 7 is "I shipped
the project and I love this," you've de-risked a bigger commitment (like a
short bootcamp or a full job search) with real evidence instead of a hunch. If
the answer is "I'm exhausted and I dread it," you found that out for the cost of
some evenings, not your savings.
