# When NOT to switch careers: running away vs. running toward

Most career-change advice is cheerleading. This is the counterweight. Some
switches are a mistake, and the people who most need to hear it are usually the
ones most committed to not hearing it. Here are the honest signs that a switch
might be the wrong move — or at least the wrong move *right now*.

## You're running away, not toward

This is the big one. There's a crucial difference between being pulled *toward*
a new field because the work genuinely calls to you, and being pushed *away*
from your current job because you're miserable in it. They feel identical from
the inside, and they lead to completely different outcomes.

If you're running *toward* software — you love the actual work, you'd do it on a
desert island, the new field's daily texture excites you — then a switch has a
real target. If you're running *away* from boredom, a bad manager, stagnant pay,
or a field that's lost its shine, the danger is that *any* escape looks like
salvation. You may not want software specifically; you may just want *out*. And
if that's the case, you could spend $14k and a year of upheaval landing in a new
field only to discover the restlessness followed you, because the thing you
actually needed was a different role, a different company, or simply a change —
not that *specific* change.

The diagnostic: if your current field magically became interesting and
well-paid again tomorrow, would the pull toward software vanish? If yes, you're
mostly running away. If the pull would remain, it's real.

## The timing is genuinely wrong

A switch can be right in the abstract and wrong this year. Signs the timing is
off: you have no financial cushion and the plan requires one; a new baby, a
family health crisis, or another major life stressor is already consuming your
bandwidth; your partner is not actually on board and you're planning to
steamroll that. "Right idea, wrong year" is a real category, and "wait twelve
months and do it from a position of strength" is often wiser than "leap now
because the frustration is acute today."

## You're idealizing the destination

Every field looks better from outside. If your image of software is "creative
flow, high pay, respect" and contains no standups, no legacy code, no on-call,
no rejection, you're in love with a postcard, not the place. People who switch
based on the postcard are the ones blindsided when the real job turns out to be
a job. Make sure you've looked at the unglamorous 60% before you commit.

## You haven't tested the cheap hypotheses first

If you've never shipped a real project, never experienced the work as work, and
never talked to people actually doing it — and you're already reaching for the
$14k irreversible option — you're skipping the cheap experiments that could
confirm or kill the idea for a fraction of the cost. That's not boldness, it's
impatience wearing boldness's clothes.

## The honest reframe

None of these is necessarily a *no*. They're a *not like this* or a *not yet*.
The strongest version of a career switch is one where you've separated the pull
from the push, confirmed the pull is real and specific, tested it cheaply,
brought your family genuinely along, and timed it from stability rather than
desperation. If you can't yet say those things, the responsible move may be to
do the work that makes them true — not to abandon the switch, but to earn the
right to make it well.
