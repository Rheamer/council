# Burnout vs. boredom: how to tell which one you have

"I need to leave my job" is a conclusion. It is not a diagnosis. Before you act
on it — especially before you spend savings and upend your family to act on
it — it's worth figuring out *what* you're actually trying to escape. Because
two very different conditions produce the identical sentence, and they call for
opposite responses.

## The two conditions

**Burnout** is depletion. You're exhausted, cynical, and running on empty. The
work may even be objectively good, but you have nothing left to bring to it.
Burnout is about *capacity* — the tank is dry. Critically, burnout follows you.
If you're burned out and you switch jobs without addressing the depletion, you
will be burned out in the new job within months, because the problem traveled
with you.

**Boredom** is the opposite of depletion — it's *under-stimulation*. You have
plenty of capacity; the work simply doesn't engage it. You're not exhausted,
you're restless. You find yourself energized by anything *other* than the job —
a side project, a hobby, learning something new at midnight. Boredom is about
*fit* — the work isn't using the part of you that wants to be used.

## The diagnostic question

Here's the cleanest test: **When you do the thing you think you want to switch
to, how do you feel?**

If you come home drained from your job and *also* can't summon energy for the
new interest — if the side project feels like one more obligation — that points
toward burnout. The issue is your reserves, not your career.

If you come home drained from your job but then light up the moment you open
the side project — if you'll happily code until 1am after a full day you
described as exhausting — that's not depletion. That's boredom plus genuine
pull toward something else. The energy is there; it's just being spent
somewhere your job isn't.

The person who grins at their laptop debugging a personal project at midnight,
after a day they described as soul-deadening, almost certainly has a *boredom
and fit* problem, not a burnout problem. The tank isn't empty; it's being
filled by the wrong activity.

## Why the distinction changes the plan

- If it's **burnout**, switching careers is a expensive way to avoid the real
  fix. Rest, boundaries, addressing the depletion's source — these come first.
  A new career undertaken from a burned-out state inherits the burnout. Solve
  the energy problem before making the irreversible decision.

- If it's **boredom and poor fit**, then "stay and push through" is the trap.
  No amount of grit makes under-stimulating work stimulating. The responsible
  move is to find work that engages the capacity you clearly still have — which
  may well mean a change, executed deliberately.

## The complicating overlap

Real life is messy: chronic boredom in a job you can't leave *causes* a slow,
grinding burnout over time. Someone can have both — bored for years, and now
also depleted by the futility of it. In that case, address the depletion enough
to think clearly, but don't mistake "I recovered some energy" for "the boredom
is solved." The fit problem will still be there, patiently, when you're rested.
The honest reckoning is to separate the two threads and treat each on its own
terms, rather than letting a single overloaded sentence — "I need to leave" —
make a life-sized decision on incomplete information.
