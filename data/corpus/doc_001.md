# Mid-career transitions into tech: what the outcomes data actually says

Every few months another viral thread claims that anyone can "learn to code"
and triple their salary in a year. And every few months a counter-thread
insists the bootcamp era is dead and nobody hires juniors anymore. Both are
selling something. The data is more boring and more useful than either.

Let's define the population carefully, because it matters: people in their
early-to-mid thirties, already employed in a technical-adjacent field
(engineering, finance, data-heavy operations), making a deliberate switch into
software. Not 22-year-olds. Not people fleeing a field they hated. People with
a real job, a mortgage, and often kids.

**Placement rates are real but slower than advertised.** Reputable bootcamps
with audited outcomes (the ones that publish CIRR-style reports) tend to show
60-75% of graduates employed in a related role within six months, and
80-90% within twelve. The gap between "six months" and "twelve months" is the
part the marketing glosses over. For a career changer with a family, that
extra six months of job searching — unpaid, anxious — is the real cost, more
than tuition.

**Prior technical background helps more than people expect.** Switchers who
already think in systems — engineers, especially — place faster and report
higher first-year satisfaction than switchers from non-technical fields. The
mental model of "decompose a problem, reason about constraints, debug
methodically" transfers. You are not starting from zero; you are relearning
where to point an existing skill.

**Age is a smaller barrier than the anxious assume, but not zero.** Surveys of
hiring managers consistently rank a working portfolio and the ability to
communicate above age or degree pedigree. That said, the *first* job is the
hard one. After you have 18 months of professional software experience, the
"no CS degree, switched at 34" framing simply evaporates from the
conversation. The credential question is a first-door problem, not a
career-long one.

**The salary story is bimodal.** Some switchers take a step *down* in their
first tech role — a junior title at a salary at or below what they left.
Within two to three years, the trajectory typically overtakes their old field,
because software comp grows faster early-career. But the first-year number can
sting, and people who only modeled the eventual upside, not the dip, are the
ones who feel ambushed. Model the dip.

**Self-reported regret is low — with an asterisk.** Among people who completed
a switch, regret is rare; most describe a hard first year followed by
durable satisfaction. But this is survivorship data. It does not include the
people who started a bootcamp, didn't place, and quietly went back to their old
field. Those people exist and rarely write reflective essays.

The honest summary: a mid-career switch into tech, for someone with a
technical background and a realistic financial runway, has a good but not
guaranteed expected value. The biggest predictors of a good outcome are a
shipped portfolio project, a real network rather than cold applications, and
enough financial cushion to survive a job search that takes longer than the
brochure promised. The biggest predictor of a bad outcome is underestimating
the timeline and running out of money or morale before the door opens.
