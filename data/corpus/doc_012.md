# Salary negotiation for first-job-in-tech career changers

Negotiation advice written for experienced engineers doesn't fit a career
changer landing their first software role. Your leverage is different, your
anxieties are different, and the most common mistakes are different. Here's the
version for someone making the jump.

## Reframe your starting point

The instinct of a career changer is to feel they're starting from zero and
should be grateful for any offer. That instinct will cost you money. You are not
a 22-year-old new grad. You're an adult who has spent years being professionally
reliable, managing complexity, and communicating with stakeholders in a
demanding field. Those don't show up as "years of software experience," but they
show up as *value* — and they're a legitimate part of what you're negotiating
on.

## Expect the first number to sting (and plan for it)

For many career changers, the first software offer is at or slightly below their
previous salary. If you were earning, say, in the high $90ks in another
engineering field, a first junior-dev offer in the $70k-$90k range is common and
not an insult — it reflects that you're junior *in this field*, even if senior
in life. The mistake is to either (a) be so wounded by the number that you
reject a good opportunity, or (b) be so grateful that you take the first offer
without a word. Neither serves you. Know your floor (the number below which the
switch doesn't make financial sense for your household) before you ever
interview, so the emotional moment of the offer doesn't make the decision for
you.

## The first job is about trajectory, not peak salary

Early-career software compensation grows fast. The gap between a $78k offer and
an $85k offer matters far less than landing somewhere that will *teach you* and
give you real experience to put on the résumé in eighteen months. Optimize the
first job for learning, mentorship, and a codebase you'll grow in — that
trajectory is worth far more than squeezing the last few thousand out of the
starting number. Negotiate, but don't torpedo a great learning environment over
a marginal salary difference.

## How to actually negotiate the offer

- **Always negotiate, politely, once.** A simple "I'm excited about this. Based
  on my background and the value I'll bring, is there flexibility on the base?"
  costs nothing and frequently yields a few thousand dollars. Employers expect
  it and rarely rescind over a respectful ask.
- **Negotiate the whole package, not just base.** Sign-on bonus, start date, PTO,
  remote flexibility, a professional-development budget, an early review with a
  raise tied to it — these are all levers, and some are easier for an employer to
  grant than base salary.
- **Use your domain as leverage where it's real.** If you're switching into a
  company that benefits from your old field's knowledge (e.g., an
  automotive-engineer moving into mobility or manufacturing software), that
  overlap is rare and valuable — name it. You're not a generic junior; you're a
  junior who closes a specific gap.
- **Get competing signal if you can.** Even one other process in flight changes
  the conversation. You don't need a bidding war; you need to not be negotiating
  against yourself.

## The mindset that wins

Negotiate from the assumption that you're a reasonable professional discussing a
fair arrangement — not a supplicant hoping not to be found out. The imposter
voice will tell you to take whatever you're given and be quiet. That voice is
expensive. A calm, single, well-framed ask, anchored in the real value your
background brings, is the move — and then a decision based on trajectory and
fit, not just the starting digit.
