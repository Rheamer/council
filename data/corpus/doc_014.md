# A practical decision-making framework for high-stakes, uncertain choices

Big, irreversible-feeling decisions tend to get made badly — not because people
are foolish, but because the decisions are emotionally loud, and loudness
crowds out structure. Here is a framework for thinking clearly when the stakes
are high and the answer isn't obvious. It won't tell you what to choose. It will
keep you from choosing for the wrong reasons.

## 1. Separate the decision from the feeling driving it

Strong emotion is information, but it's not a conclusion. "I'm frustrated and I
need to change something" is a feeling. "I should quit my job for a bootcamp" is
a decision. The feeling is real and worth honoring; it does not automatically
license that specific decision. Name the feeling, then ask what range of actions
could address it — usually it's more than one.

## 2. Reframe "reversible vs. irreversible"

Decisions feel binary and permanent under stress. Most aren't. Before treating a
choice as a one-way door, ask: *what's actually reversible here, and what isn't?*
Often the scary option can be restructured into a reversible experiment. "Quit
and do a bootcamp" feels like a one-way door. "Spend three months shipping a
real project while employed, and decide based on what I learn" is the same
direction through a door you can walk back through. Convert one-way doors into
two-way doors wherever the structure allows.

## 3. Make the constraints and the floor explicit

Write down the hard constraints — the things that must remain true no matter
what. Financial floor (the savings level you won't go below). Relationship floor
(the commitments to people who depend on you). Time floor. A decision that
violates a genuine floor isn't bold, it's just a different problem you'll be
solving later. Constraints aren't the enemy of ambition; they're the boundaries
that let you be ambitious without being reckless.

## 4. Define what success and failure actually look like

Vague goals produce vague decisions. "I want to be happier" can't be evaluated.
"Within 12 months I want a shipped project, a clear read on whether I like the
work as work, and a real network" can. Equally important: define the *stopping
rule* up front — the condition under which you'll conclude this isn't working and
take the fallback. Deciding the exit criteria before you're emotionally invested
is far easier than deciding them mid-struggle.

## 5. Seek disconfirming evidence, not reassurance

Under the pull of a decision you want to make, you'll unconsciously collect
evidence that supports it and discount the rest. Deliberately invert this. Ask:
*what would have to be true for this to be a mistake? What's the strongest
honest case against it?* Talk to someone who'll tell you no. The goal isn't to
talk yourself out of it; it's to make sure that if you proceed, you're doing it
with the objections fully heard rather than suppressed.

## 6. Run the cheap experiments before the expensive commitment

Almost every big decision contains a smaller, cheaper version that produces most
of the information at a fraction of the cost. Before the irreversible spend, what
$0 or low-cost test would tell you most of what you need to know? Doing that test
first is not procrastination — it's buying down the uncertainty on the expensive
choice.

## 7. Bring the stakeholders in as partners

If the decision affects other people, they are not obstacles to your decision;
they are co-owners of it. Decisions made *with* the people they affect are more
robust, better-informed, and survive hard stretches that unilateral decisions
don't.

## The point

This framework rarely outputs a clean "do X." More often it transforms a loud,
scary, all-or-nothing dilemma into a sequence of smaller, clearer, more
reversible steps — each of which you can take, evaluate, and adjust. That
transformation, not a verdict, is usually what a good decision process actually
produces.
