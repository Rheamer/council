# Bootcamp vs. self-study vs. CS degree: an honest comparison

There is no universally correct path into software. There is only the path
that fits your money, your time, your discipline, and your tolerance for
ambiguity. Here is the trade-off space without the sales pitch.

## The CS degree

A second bachelor's or a master's (e.g., an online MSCS) is the most expensive
and slowest option — two to four years and tens of thousands of dollars. It is
also the most legible to conservative employers and the most thorough on
fundamentals. For someone who already holds a technical degree and just needs
to redirect, a full second degree is usually overkill. The exception is people
targeting roles where the credential is a hard filter (some large enterprises,
certain visa situations, research-adjacent work). For most career switchers,
the ROI doesn't justify the years.

## The bootcamp

A full-time immersive bootcamp compresses job-readiness into three to six
months. The pitch is speed and structure: a curriculum, deadlines, peers,
career services, and a forcing function. For someone who struggles to stay
disciplined alone, that structure is worth real money.

The costs are also real. Tuition runs $10k-$20k. Full-time programs require you
to *stop earning* for the duration, which for a primary earner is often a far
bigger cost than tuition itself. Quality varies wildly; a bad bootcamp is
$15k for a Udemy course with extra steps. The good ones publish audited
outcomes — if a program won't show you placement data, that silence is the
data.

A note on ISAs (income-share agreements): "pay nothing up front, pay a
percentage of salary after you're hired above a threshold." This shifts risk
off the up-front balance sheet, which is attractive if cash is tight. But read
the fine print: the income percentage, the salary floor that triggers
payments, the payment cap, and the term length. A poorly capped ISA can cost
far more than tuition would have. ISAs are a financing instrument, not a free
lunch.

## Self-study

The cheapest path and the one with the highest variance in outcomes. Online
courses, free curricula, and personal projects cost almost nothing and let you
keep your job and income. People with strong self-discipline and a clear plan
do brilliantly. People without external structure tend to stall — they finish
the eleventh tutorial, never ship a real project, and circle for years in
"tutorial purgatory."

The hidden cost of self-study isn't money, it's *time and momentum*. Studying
six scattered hours a week around a full-time job and a kid, you can absolutely
get there — but the timeline stretches to eighteen months or two years, and
burnout is a live risk. The slow path is not a safe path; it's a path with a
different risk profile (stall and exhaustion instead of money and disruption).

## How to actually choose

Match the path to your real constraint:

- **Discipline is your bottleneck** → bootcamp buys structure.
- **Cash and family stability are your bottleneck** → self-study protects them, at the cost of time.
- **A specific employer's credential filter is your bottleneck** → consider the degree.

And a hybrid that many switchers underrate: self-study *until you have shipped
one real project and confirmed you like the work*, then a short bootcamp or
focused job-search sprint to close the gap. You don't have to pick the whole
staircase before you take the first step.
