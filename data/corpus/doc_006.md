# Software engineering market conditions, 2024: a Midwest and Detroit view

The national headlines about tech hiring are written from San Francisco and
Seattle. If you live in Detroit, those headlines describe a different planet
than the one you're job-hunting on. Here is the regional picture for 2024.

## The national backdrop, briefly

After the 2021-2022 hiring frenzy and the 2023 correction, 2024 settled into
something more sober. The era of companies hiring junior engineers in bulk on
optimism has cooled. Bars are higher, especially for entry-level and
career-changer candidates, and "we'll train you up" is rarer than it was three
years ago. This is real and worth respecting. It is not, however, the "nobody
is hiring" apocalypse that doom-threads describe.

## What's different in the Midwest

The coastal boom-and-bust is muted here. Detroit and the broader Midwest never
ran up the absurd salaries, so they didn't crash as hard either. Demand is
steadier, if less explosive. The employers are different too:

- **Automotive and mobility tech.** This is the region's hidden advantage. The
  auto industry is now a software industry that happens to make cars —
  infotainment, ADAS, embedded systems, fleet telematics, manufacturing
  software, supply-chain tooling. Companies in and around Detroit need software
  people who *also understand vehicles and manufacturing*. That intersection is
  not crowded.
- **Enterprise and "boring" software.** Insurance, healthcare systems, banking,
  logistics, B2B SaaS. Less glamorous than a consumer startup, more stable,
  and consistently hiring. These shops often value reliability and
  communication over leetcode wizardry.
- **A real but small startup scene.** Detroit's startup community is growing
  but modest. Good for networking and learning; not where most of the jobs are.

## Salary reality

Midwest software salaries run below coastal numbers but so does the cost of
living, and the ratio is favorable. A first software role for a career changer
in metro Detroit might land somewhere in the $70k-$95k range depending on the
employer and the candidate's portfolio; mid-level roles climb into the
six-figure range; senior roles comfortably exceed it. For someone currently
earning in the high $90ks in another field, the *first* tech job may be lateral
or even a slight step down — but the slope afterward is steeper than in mature,
slow-growth fields.

## Remote complicates the picture

Remote roles let Midwest candidates compete for higher coastal-adjacent
salaries — but also force them to compete against a national applicant pool,
which is brutal for a first job. The realistic move for a career changer is
often to land the *first* role locally or hybrid, where domain background and
in-person networking count for more, and only later leverage remote
opportunities once you have professional experience on the résumé.

## The strategic takeaway for a Detroit-based switcher

The market is tougher than 2021 but far from closed, and the region offers a
specific edge: the overlap between traditional engineering/automotive domain
knowledge and software demand. A candidate who can credibly say "I understand
both how this physical product works *and* how to build the software around it"
is solving a hiring problem that pure-software juniors can't. That is a sharper
positioning than competing as a generic entry-level web developer against a
national remote pool.
