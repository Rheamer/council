# User Knowledge Base — Mark Chen

*Prepared as a confidential career-coaching dossier. Sources: three intake
sessions, a review of Mark's self-study logs, and his own journaling notes.*

## background

Mark Chen is 34 years old and lives in Royal Oak, a suburb just north of
Detroit, Michigan. He grew up in the Detroit metro area and never left it —
something he describes with a mix of comfort and restlessness.

He earned a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering from Michigan State University in
2013 and went to work almost immediately for a tier-1 automotive supplier,
where he has now spent eleven years. He designs suspension components —
control arms, knuckles, and most recently wheel hubs. He is good at it. He
was promoted to Senior Mechanical Engineer roughly four years ago and has
been in essentially the same role since.

Mark is methodical and detail-oriented, the kind of engineer who reads the
spec twice before touching the CAD. He is risk-averse by temperament and by
circumstance: he is the primary earner for a family of three, and he weighs
their stability heavily in every decision. He is also, by his own admission,
"increasingly frustrated" — a word that comes up repeatedly in his notes.

For the past eight months he has been teaching himself to code on evenings
and weekends. He started with Python, worked through a freeCodeCamp track and
a paid Udemy full-stack course, and has been building small web projects. He
is currently most comfortable in Python and has been picking up Django on the
backend and a bit of React on the front end. He describes the act of coding
as the most absorbed he's felt in years.

## current_situation

Mark's base salary is $98,000, with an annual bonus that has run between
$5,000 and $8,000 in recent years. That bonus has felt less reliable lately;
two rounds of layoffs have swept through adjacent teams in the past 18 months,
and while Mark survived both, the anxiety lingers. He notes that salary growth
in automotive engineering "stagnated years ago" — his raises have tracked
inflation at best.

His manager, Dave, is an automotive lifer in his late fifties — a genuinely
good guy who has been at the company longer than Mark has been alive,
practically. Dave likes Mark and has hinted at a path toward a lead engineer
role in a year or two. He also, by Mark's account, "doesn't get" why anyone
would want to leave a stable engineering job, and Mark has not told him about
the coding. There is no animosity; there is just a quiet sense that Dave's
world and the world Mark is reaching toward don't overlap.

Day to day, Mark is bored. He told his coach he "hasn't enjoyed designing
wheel hubs for four years." The work is competent, the work is fine, and the
work no longer engages him. The contrast with how he feels when debugging a
Python script late at night is stark, and that contrast is most of what is
driving this whole question.

## goals

What Mark says he wants, in roughly his own words:

- To do work that engages him intellectually the way coding currently does.
- To increase his earning potential. He has friends in tech earning 1.5x to
  2x his salary, and he is acutely aware of the gap.
- To feel some control over his career trajectory rather than waiting for the
  next reorg to decide his fate.
- Not to blow up his family's financial stability in the process.

What he has *not* clearly articulated, and what is worth probing: whether he
wants to be a software engineer, or whether he wants to *not be a mechanical
engineer*. These are not the same goal, and Mark has not separated them. He
also has not defined what "success" would look like 12 months out — is it a
job offer, a salary number, a feeling?

## constraints

- **Financial runway.** The family has roughly $42,000 in liquid savings (the
  401k is separate and he considers it untouchable). The mortgage is
  $1,750/month. A six-month, $14,000 bootcamp would consume both tuition and a
  meaningful chunk of runway if he stopped working.
- **Time.** Mark has a demanding full-time job and a three-year-old. His study
  time is genuinely scarce — evenings after Ethan is down, and fragments of
  weekends. This caps how fast self-study can move.
- **Age and credential anxiety.** Mark is 34 and has no CS degree. He
  repeatedly voices doubt about whether companies will hire someone with his
  profile. Whether this is a real market constraint or an internal story is an
  open question.
- **Family bandwidth.** Lisa works part-time and carries most of the childcare
  load already. Any plan that further reduces Mark's availability at home has a
  real cost that lands on her.
- **Risk tolerance.** Mark is constitutionally cautious. A plan that is
  financially sound but psychologically reckless (for him) is not actually a
  fit, and vice versa.

## relationships

**Lisa (35)** is Mark's wife, a part-time graphic designer earning around
$35,000 a year. Her support is real but qualified. She has said she'll "support
whatever he decides" — but Mark noted that the way she said it "was tight." She
is anxious about the finances, and reasonably so: she has the clearest view of
the household budget. She is not opposed to the switch; she is opposed to
recklessness. Any plan needs her genuine buy-in, not just her permission.

**Ethan (3)** is their son. Childcare is a major line item and a major
constraint on both parents' time. He is the reason "stability" is not an
abstraction for Mark.

**Lisa's parents** live about 20 minutes away and provide regular childcare
help. This is a meaningful asset — it is part of why the family functions on
1.5 incomes — and it is also a reason Mark feels rooted in the Detroit area.

**Dave**, Mark's manager (see *current_situation*), is supportive of Mark the
employee but is not a confidant on the career question.

**Raj**, a friend/acquaintance, transitioned into tech at 36. Raj told Mark the
first year was "brutal" but that he has no regrets and is now at a senior level
at a large tech company. Raj is Mark's main real-world data point that the
switch is possible — and possibly an outlier he is over-weighting.

The headline tension: the bootcamp's $14k tuition is affordable on paper, but
the family's margin is thin. If Mark left his job to attend full-time, the
household would drop to Lisa's ~$35k while burning savings on both living costs
and tuition. Six months of that could consume most of the $42k cushion. If he
keeps working and studies part-time, the money is safe but the timeline
stretches and the burnout risk rises. Mark has written that the math is "tight
but possible," which is accurate and also conveniently vague.

## decision_context

The decision Mark is wrestling with: **whether to switch from mechanical
engineering into software development, and if so, how.**

He has framed three options:

1. **Full-time bootcamp** — ~$14,000, six months, immersive. Fastest path to
   job-readiness, highest financial and family strain. Two programs are on his
   radar: a fictional local immersive called "Codesmith Detroit," and a
   deferred-tuition / ISA-style program he refers to as "the Lambda-style ISA"
   where you pay a share of income after you land a job.
2. **Continued self-study while keeping the job** — lowest financial risk,
   preserves stability, but slow, and competing with a full-time job and a
   toddler for his attention. Risk of indefinite stall.
3. **Stay in mechanical engineering** — accept the boredom, lean into the
   possible lead-engineer path with Dave, keep everything stable.

Genuinely open questions a good advisor (or agent) would want to resolve:

- Is Mark running *toward* software or *away from* automotive boredom? His
  answer changes which option is right.
- Is the credential/age concern a real hiring barrier in the 2024 Detroit /
  Midwest market, or an imposter-syndrome story?
- Does Lisa's "tight" support survive contact with a concrete plan, or does it
  fracture?
- Does Mark actually enjoy software *as a job* (deadlines, code review,
  on-call, legacy code) versus as an evening hobby?
- What is the minimum viable plan that tests the switch without betting the
  family's stability on it?

There is no obvious right answer here. A reasonable advisor could land on any
of the three options depending on how they weight financial caution,
psychological fit, and Mark's tolerance for ambiguity. The situation is
deliberately balanced.

## prior_consultations

Mark has informally run this question past several people before. The notes
below capture what came back — kept here precisely *because* they did not
agree, so a fresh advisory pass starts from a real spread of opinion rather
than a settled answer.

- **A friend who switched into tech (Raj).** Strongly pro-switch. "Just build
  and ship something real, the credential won't matter." Emphasized that the
  first year is brutal but survivable. Discounted the financial fear as
  short-term. Mark trusts him but suspects he's an outlier and may be
  generalizing from one lucky path.
- **His manager (Dave).** Implicitly pro-stay. Doesn't know about the coding;
  floated a lead-engineer path as if the answer to restlessness is more
  responsibility in the same field. Sincere, but reasoning entirely from inside
  the automotive world.
- **Lisa.** Conditional. Not opposed to the switch, opposed to recklessness.
  Her "support whatever you decide" is real but anxious; her actual position is
  "show me a plan that doesn't put us at risk." She has the clearest view of the
  household budget and the least appetite for the immersive-bootcamp version.
- **A one-off chat with a financial-minded acquaintance.** Pure numbers view:
  "the bootcamp is affordable on paper but your runway has no shock absorber —
  prove the income side before you spend the cushion." Said nothing about
  whether Mark would *like* the work.

No consensus emerged. The optimist, the incumbent, the partner, and the
numbers-person each weighted a different axis (upside vs. stability vs. family
risk vs. cash flow), which is exactly why Mark still feels stuck. A useful
council should expect to reproduce that same tension before resolving it —
not assume it away.
